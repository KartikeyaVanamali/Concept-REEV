XEV 9S REEV Concept

A range-extended electric SUV derived from the XEV 9S INGLO platform. Electric at the wheels at all times. Engine-generator with no mechanical path to either axle. This page holds the full concept — platform derivation, control laws, cost model, and simulation.

Range-extended electric · XEV 9S INGLO platform · Concept
What if the XEV 9S could go anywhere?
A purpose-redesign of the XEV 9S INGLO platform for range-extended electric duty.

Mahindra's XEV 9S is one of the best electric SUVs built in India. Its one limitation is the same limitation every BEV faces: the charging network decides how far you can go, and how long you will wait. This concept asks a single engineering question — what is the minimum surgical change to the XEV 9S platform to remove that limitation permanently, without compromising what the XEV 9S already does well?

Range-extended electric vehicles are not a new idea. Nissan's e-Power proved fixed-point generator operation and always-electric drive at volume — the same architectural principle, without the plug-in battery. Li Auto, Leapmotor and Aito scaled the plug-in REEV with grid charging. What this concept does is apply that architecture deliberately to an Indian context — right-sized high-power phosphate pack, a 105 kW generator genuinely capable of sustained highway support, six pre-validated fixed operating bands with a smallest-sufficient selection law, and a control law that treats battery protection, generator hysteresis, auxiliary shedding, and regen acceptance as one interacting system — all argued from first principles, on an existing production platform, without a clean-sheet vehicle or an 800 V architecture.

The market case — who buys this and why

This concept is not designed to convert the BEV believer. It is designed to remove every reason the ICE buyer gives for not buying electric.

  • Range anxiety: eliminated. 42 L of petrol at ~14–15 km/l generator efficiency gives 580–630 km of petrol-only range — on top of the 200–255 km real-world EV range already in the battery. Combined system range on a full charge and full tank is ~800–885 km without any charging stop. The combined system range on a full charge and full tank exceeds any ICE competitor in the segment.
  • Charging infrastructure: irrelevant for the daily commute. City driving is electric. Long trips use petrol stations — every one of which already exists.
  • Petrol running cost: a premium petrol SUV in this class returns 8–10 km/l in real-world city conditions. At current Mumbai pump prices that is roughly ₹10–13 per city kilometre. The same REEV route on electricity at approximately 25 kWh/100 km and ₹8/kWh costs roughly ₹2 per kilometre — one-fifth of the petrol bill for the identical daily commute. The petrol buyer is paying the highest per-km rate at the worst efficiency point of the engine for the most common use case.
  • Diesel — DPF and the 10-year clock: BS6-II diesel SUVs carry a Diesel Particulate Filter that requires periodic forced regeneration and eventual replacement at an exorbitant cost on a premium vehicle. AdBlue/DEF consumption adds ₹2,000–₹4,000 per service interval. Beyond the maintenance cost, Supreme Court and NGT orders prohibit diesel vehicles older than 10 years from operating in Delhi NCR and other metros. A diesel SUV bought today becomes unregisterable in its original city before many buyers finish paying for it. No operational age limit applies to electric-drive vehicles. The diesel buyer is paying a premium for fuel economy that comes with a hidden expiry date and a growing maintenance schedule.
  • The strong hybrid alternative: strong hybrids improve city fuel economy but are taxed as conventional vehicles at the full rate applicable to their size — 40% GST for an over-4-metre SUV at current rates. A premium strong hybrid in this class carries mechanical complexity (planetary gearset, dedicated motor-generator, separate small battery pack) without removing petrol dependence or providing meaningful all-electric range for a daily commute. The hybrid buyer pays the ICE premium without the ICE escape route.
  • Performance: uncompromised. Wheel torque is always electric. Engine sound at cruise is calm and calibrated to fixed bands — not a continuously revving petrol engine chasing demand.
  • Fuel flexibility: the buyer who is range-anxious today keeps using petrol. The buyer who installs a home charger tomorrow discovers they almost never visit a petrol station. The car works the same either way — it adapts to the owner rather than requiring the owner to adapt to it.

Nissan's e-Power proved in Japan that fixed-point generator operation and always-electric drive works at volume — albeit without a plug-in battery or meaningful EV range. The e-Power's tiny buffer battery is never grid-charged; the architecture this concept builds on extends that proof of concept with a 40/50 kWh plug-in pack sized for real daily EV use. Japan's short-trip market validated the generator-as-powerplant idea; India's driving profile has the same short-trip city core plus long-distance inter-city runs that BEVs struggle with. This REEV concept is the architecture that fits both without asking the owner to choose. In body and proportion it would look like the XUV 7XO — the ICE cousin of the XEV 9S — but it carries the XEV 9S's electric platform underneath.

The diesel argument — and where this wins

Diesel's last remaining moat is highway fuel economy. On a long run at 100–120 km/h, a well-calibrated 2.2 mHawk diesel returns 14–15 km/l, and no BEV can match that convenience when chargers are sparse. The REEV attacks that moat from two different angles depending on the trip profile.

In the city, the comparison is not even close. The REEV runs on electricity. A diesel runs on diesel in the city, at 10–11 km/l with full particulate and NOx load. The REEV buyer pays grid rate for city commuting; the diesel buyer pays pump rate at the worst efficiency point of the engine.

On the highway, a pure generator run — no planned charging, driving straight through — puts the REEV in the same efficiency neighbourhood as the diesel. The generator at the 55–70 kW steady bands operates at 35–39% brake thermal efficiency; the diesel at similar cruise loads runs in a similar range. The generator holds a fixed band calibrated for efficiency; the diesel sweeps its map continuously. Neither decisively beats the other on fuel cost per kilometre in this scenario alone.

The efficiency advantage on the highway becomes real when planned charging is incorporated. A mixed trip — city legs at grid cost, highway legs where the battery starts partial — brings the effective energy cost per kilometre well below diesel, because a significant fraction of the kilometres are covered at electricity rates. The buyer who charges at home overnight and at a workplace or rest-stop charger on a highway trip sees the full cost advantage across the round trip. The diesel driver pays pump rate for every kilometre.

Add V2L export — 230 V AC from the bidirectional OBC while stationary — and the REEV functions as a home battery backup during grid outages, supplying household loads from the traction battery until the 15% SoC floor, with no generator running. No diesel SUV offers that without engine noise, exhaust, or fuel consumption. The REEV concept is not a claim of free highway efficiency. It is a claim that for the typical owner — short daily trips on electricity, occasional long runs on petrol with en-route charging — the total annual fuel bill is structurally lower than diesel, without asking the owner to change how they drive.

How this holds as BEV packs grow

BEV packs will grow. 100 kWh is already standard in the segment above this. 120 and 160 kWh variants are in production elsewhere. The usual counter-argument to a REEV is that a large-enough BEV makes it unnecessary. The counter-argument has a flaw: the REEV tracks at half the pack size every generation, so the cost gap in its favour widens, not narrows, as energy density improves.

That is why the concept does not become obsolete when BEV packs grow: the REEV pack simply scales to half of whatever the leading BEV carries, always cheaper, always with the safety net.

Why half the battery — and which battery

The XEV 9S carries 79 kWh because it has to. Every kilometre past the charge point must come from cells. The REEV has a generator for that job, so the pack only needs to cover three duties: daily city use without the engine starting, transient power above what the generator can sustain, and the Turtle reserve for emergency mobility.

City-only pure-EV range on 40 kWh is approximately 180–200 km at ARAI-equivalent conditions, with ~340 km MIDC certified range. For the vast majority of Indian daily driving patterns — median trip 35–50 km — the engine never starts on most workdays. The 79 kWh surplus that delivers 679 km MIDC (410–510 km real-world) adds cost and mass without adding a use case that the generator does not already cover more efficiently on a long run.

Cell family: BYD Short Blade 2.0 high-power phosphate

The XEV 9S uses BYD's Blade 1.0 LFP — a cell optimised for maximum cycle life at the cost of power density. For this concept the preferred cell family is BYD's Short Blade 2.0 in its high-power PHEV-class variant. The exact cathode chemistry (LFP or LMFP) is an evidence boundary — early production regulatory analysis still describes LFP, while some launch material describes LMFP at ~3.8 V nominal. The architecture works with either; the series cell count differs between the two cases. Three properties are decisive regardless of which cathode is confirmed:

  • High-power phosphate duty cycle. The REEV requires approximately 4.67C peak from the 40 kWh pack and 5.26C from the 50 kWh pack — roughly double the C-rate the Blade 1.0 sees in the XEV 9S. The Short Blade 2.0 platform is specifically described by BYD as its high-power member, designed for PHEV shallow cycling, high pulse power, and repeated generator charging. The Blade 1.0 was not designed for this duty.
  • Voltage window compatibility. The 400–465 V INGLO architecture is retained. The LFP case uses 128 cells in series (same count as the XEV 9S inference) at ~3.20 V, landing at ~409.6 V nominal. The conditional LMFP case uses ~108 cells at ~3.80 V, also landing near 409.6 V. Both cases keep the same HV bus, inverter, OBC, and charger without requalification.
  • Thermal stability. Both LFP and LMFP share the olivine crystal structure — thermal runaway onset above 270°C regardless of which cathode is confirmed. The pack can sit charged in a hot Indian summer without the calendar degradation that NMC variants show at these conditions.

Available mass optimisation paths — beyond the programme targets
  • Purpose-built REEV floor and pack enclosure (15–40 kg): the XEV 9S floor was designed around a 79 kWh pack. A purpose-built shorter enclosure, co-designed cross-members, and integrated cold plates eliminate the structural redundancy carried by the BEV platform. The pack enclosure becomes a validated structural contributor rather than a secondary addition.
  • Close-coupled generator system (10–20 kg): engine, PMSG coupling, rectifier, exhaust, after-treatment, and dedicated cooling integrated as one unit rather than individually mounted and plumbed reduces mounts, pipe lengths, wiring runs, and fluid volume.
  • Generator coupling — single reduction gear only (5–10 kg saved vs multi-gear assumption): this concept needs only a fixed-ratio coupling or single reduction gear between crankshaft and PMSG to match engine RPM to the PMSG efficiency sweet spot. The Nissan e-Power unit (~260 kg) included a traction motor, a reduction gear, and a step-up gear for wheel drive. Those do not exist in this generator system — the traction motor is the INGLO rear EDU already in the baseline. This distinction alone saves approximately 60–80 kg compared with any estimate that used the Nissan unit as a reference.
  • Selective aluminium in chassis components (10–20 kg): front subframe, suspension knuckles, control arms, and crash beams where lifecycle cost and repairability justify it. This is not a blanket aluminium claim — it requires validation at each component.
  • Cell-to-pack integration (8–15 kg): eliminating module-level housing and using the blade cell geometry's structural contribution directly reduces pack mass for the same electrical content.
  • Chassis and cabin fine-tuning (10–20 kg): hollow anti-roll bars, flow-formed 19-inch wheel baseline, topology-optimised suspension links, optimised seat frames and interior structures — none of which compromise safety, crash, NVH, or durability targets.

Conservative total recoverable mass through these paths: approximately 70–120 kg. Aggressive total: approximately 120–170 kg. Executing the full programme could bring RWD to the ~2,180–2,200 kg band and AWD to ~2,360–2,400 kg. Larger claims than this typically double-count the battery right-sizing or trade away safety, durability, NVH, cost, or repairability.

Why the Nissan e-Power is not used as a mass reference. The published ~260 kg e-Power generator unit includes the traction motor and its reduction and step-up gears for wheel drive. In this concept the traction motor is the INGLO rear EDU — a carry-over already present in the XEV 9S baseline mass, not added hardware. The generator system here connects the crankshaft to the PMSG through a fixed-ratio coupling or single reduction gear sized to match engine RPM to the PMSG efficiency optimum. There is no traction gear train on the generator side. Using the Nissan figure would therefore overestimate generator system mass by approximately 60–80 kg — the traction motor and gearing contribution to the e-Power total. The component breakdown used here reflects only what this concept's generator system physically contains.

The whole picture

A normal day, with fuel on board

Where the power goes while the generator runs

The battery is always last in the queue, so it only ever receives what is spare. That is why it charges quickly at times, slowly at others, and not at all once the generator is carrying the car on its own. Ease off there and it starts to climb again.

When the tank is empty

With no fuel on board the switch-on levels do nothing, because there is no engine to start. The car drives as a plain electric car all the way down, whichever mode you picked.

After you add fuel

You never have to tell the car you have refuelled. It notices on its own, which is why a jerry can at the roadside is enough to get moving again.

If the battery goes completely flat

Below the 0 percent on the screen the car keeps a small protected slice of charge, purely so it can wake up and crank the engine after you refuel. This chart is for the rarer case where even that slice is gone. Charge going into it is why the gauge still reads 0 percent early in a rescue, and a donor vehicle only helps if it can export power from its own socket.

Can I change mode right now?

Economy and Hills can be picked whenever you like. Sport is the only one the car can refuse, because it needs charge in hand to deliver what it promises.

Lending power to another EV

Your car treats the export load as a critical auxiliary. In an emergency rescue session, the generator starts once your SoC reaches its mode boundary so it covers what you give away rather than letting your battery drain. In a home battery backup session, the generator never starts — export draws only from the battery, stopping automatically at 15% SoC. Both sessions use the same port and the same six interlocks; only the floor condition and generator rule differ. The session ends automatically when its floor is reached — that decision is never yours to miss.

Getting a rescue from another EV

The screen reading 0 percent while the cable is in is correct, not a fault. The first energy in refills the hidden protected slice before anything shows on the gauge.

Why the car might feel slower

Low charge is the obvious reason, but not the only one. A softer pedal is almost always protection rather than a fault.

Three more things the car will tell you. While the generator runs it warns you at roughly ten minutes of fuel left, then again at roughly five. It will not start the generator while you are plugged in. And in limp-home or while rebuilding after a refuel it trims aircon and other comfort loads so the remaining energy goes into moving the car.

    Important reading rule

    Rule: Displayed SoC is an owner-facing anchor. Live BMS limits, usable kWh, weakest-cell voltage, temperature, isolation and component health always override it.

    How the states connect

    The normative transition set. Nothing outside this list is a legal move, and no transition on it is instantaneous.

    • State 1 moves to State 2 only when healthy-SoC battery capability constrains requested torque. The generator stays off.
    • State 1 or 2 moves to State 3 when protected-energy logic reaches the active 15% or 20% mode anchor, or an earlier dynamic safety boundary.
    • State 3 returns to State 1 only after protected energy and pulse reserve, the 30% or 35% target, minimum generator run and dwell, and emissions-safe shutdown are all satisfied together.
    • State 3 moves to State 4 when a sustainable battery deficit or power reserve can no longer be guaranteed while usable generator power remains.
    • State 4 returns to State 3 when battery transient and recovery capability is safely restored. It moves to State 5 only when generator power is unavailable and displayed usable energy reaches the Turtle boundary.
    • State 5 moves to State 6 only after a valid refill with complete start interlocks, or after an approved V2L rescue restores generator-start capability.
    • State 6 moves to State 3 in Economy only after the dynamic exit energy above nominal 10% is met. Any unsafe battery, fuel, generator or HV evidence instead requests a controlled stop and service.
    Rule: All transitions are bumpless. Each one preserves the current safe wheel torque first and then applies a calibrated ramp. No state change on its own opens contactors, applies the parking brake, or creates an abrupt positive or negative torque step.

    The battery is never bypassed

    A rule that sits above the state machine and constrains every state in it.

    • The traction battery stabilises the HV DC bus, accepts regeneration, supplies transients, and enables controlled generator starting and load changes. It is a live electrical component, not a fuel tank with a voltage.
    • The generator may run only while the battery, BMS, contactors, pre-charge circuit, isolation monitoring and HV safety controls all retain minimum validated function. Average battery power may approach zero in State 4, but the battery is never electrically bypassed.
    • Battery-isolated generator-only propulsion is rejected outright. No under-bonnet generator-start port, no dedicated engine starter, no second traction or black-start battery, no direct donor-to-HV connection, and no special generator-only driving state.
    • At displayed 0%, the protected physical 3% reserve normally supplies the BMS, pre-charge, pumps and controllers, generator-inverter startup, and the brief PMSG motoring pulse that cranks the engine after a valid refill.

    If even that reserve cannot start the generator

    Provided the BMS confirms every cell is healthy and inside a validated recoverable-voltage window, exactly one vehicle-to-vehicle rescue path exists: a donor EV with V2L, to 230 V AC, to an approved protected portable EVSE or rescue cable, to the normal REEV AC charge port and onboard charger, to the traction battery. The donor must explicitly support V2L, because a charge-only BEV cannot export energy, and two traction batteries must never be connected directly. At a nominal 3.3 kW supply, 10 minutes gives roughly 0.55 kWh before losses and 20 minutes roughly 1.1 kWh; recovery ends on battery capability, not on a fixed time or displayed percentage. Once available battery pulse power covers generator cranking plus startup auxiliaries plus the start factor of safety, charging terminates normally, and after safe disconnection with valid fuel, Park, brake and READY confirmation the engine starts through the PMSG and the vehicle enters State 6.

    Interlock: V2L rescue is permitted only for an energy-depleted but electrically safe pack. Cell undervoltage outside the recovery window, thermal-runaway risk, an isolation fault, crash disconnect, BMS failure, contactor or pre-charge failure, or internal pack damage all prohibit generator start and propulsion. No DC-to-DC vehicle rescue is included. The generator removes energy-depletion and charging-infrastructure anxiety; it does not conceal or bypass a genuine traction-battery safety failure.

    Generator bands

    Generator-only sustained speed (State 4): At 15% SoC the generator starts but the battery still supplements traction — both variants reach 210 km/h normally. The generator-only ceiling only applies in State 4 (near the 8% Turtle gate in Economy/Terrain; Sport adds an early-intervention step at ~10% before handing to Economy at the same 8% gate) when battery discharge is locked at zero and fuel remains. There the 105 kW generator supplies 103 kW to the traction bus (105 − 2 kW aux) = 94.8 kW at wheels, giving ~193 km/h RWD (2,580 kg) and ~192 km/h AWD (2,780 kg). The 1 km/h difference is negligible — aero dominates above 150 km/h, both variants share the same CdA, and AWD's extra 200 kg only affects rolling resistance which is under 3% of road load at that speed. Once fuel is expended, State 5 (Turtle) activates and speed falls to the Turtle floor.

    Braking rule

    Rule: Hold the generator mechanical band for a 12 s brake dwell for driveability. The rectifier stays active throughout — the 400V bus is sized for 270 kW (AWD peak traction), and max gen + regen = 143 kW = 53% of that capacity. Both sources charge the battery simultaneously during every brake event.

    Why the numbers are different

    Economy — efficiency first, generator as a quiet background task

    Economy uses the slowest demand filter: 1.5 seconds. Short power spikes from roundabouts or brief overtakes are smoothed away before they influence the generator. The generator band selection sees a settled, averaged demand and picks the smallest band that covers it. This prevents a brief hard pedal event from stepping the generator up two bands for a transient that the battery can handle alone.

    The 16-second downshift dwell is measured from when demand fell below the lower threshold, not from when the band was last changed. Driving a ghat road in Economy, the generator holds its band through a brief valley between climbs because demand only fell for a few seconds — not for 16. This avoids the band hunting that would otherwise make the generator audible every time the gradient eases.

    Sport — battery reserve held higher, generator responds faster

    Sport — higher entry bar, faster generator when it does run

    Sport requires 20% displayed SoC to engage — 5 percentage points more than Economy — because it calibrates the vehicle to deliver its maximum traction output immediately. The battery must have enough reserve to cover a repeated hard pull without the generator having yet reached its band. If SoC is below 20%, Sport is refused and the reason is shown. The selector physically stays in Sport; the car simply continues on Economy calibration until the reserve is rebuilt.

    Once engaged, the demand filter drops to 0.35 seconds — fast enough that the generator begins ramping almost as soon as a sustained hard demand is detected. The 8-second downshift dwell means the generator releases a band twice as quickly as Economy after demand eases. Combined with the higher 12 kW recovery surplus target, Sport drives the battery back toward 35% faster, keeping the high-power reserve larger.

    Sport low-energy handover: when the BMS raises its early intervention flag — nominally around 10% displayed SoC, earlier if the pack is cold, aged, or imbalanced — Sport is demoted to Economy automatically. The demotion fires on the BMS signal, not a fixed percentage, so it adjusts to actual pack condition rather than a calendar assumption. Hills/Terrain is deliberately excluded from this demotion because its power management is already Economy-like; forcing it to Economy would only remove descent regeneration priority at the point it matters most.

    Hills/Terrain — calm generator, active traction management

    Hills/Terrain — the mode that holds its band through a crest

    Hills/Terrain uses the slowest demand filter of all three modes — 2.5 seconds — and the longest downshift dwell at 24 seconds. On rolling terrain, the generator reaches a band on the climb, holds it through the brief crest where demand momentarily drops, and is still at output when the next descent begins. Economy with its 16-second dwell would have already stepped down; Terrain rides through.

    The accelerator response stays Economy-like — progressive and smooth — not the immediate linear response of Sport. This is intentional. On a slippery forest track or a steep descent, an abrupt torque change is more destabilising than a delayed one. The mode is conservative in its power delivery but proactive in its energy management.

    On descents with gradient above 3% and active braking, Hills/Terrain pauses the generator to preserve full regenerative charge acceptance. The battery absorbs the descent energy rather than fighting the generator trying to charge it at the same time. On the next climb the generator resumes. This is the downshift-dwell rule applied in reverse: the generator does not start a new band when descent regen is actively running.

    AWD front-axle assist engages automatically in Hills/Terrain when wheel slip is detected, road grade exceeds 5%, or requested power exceeds 120 kW — without requiring any driver input. The front EDU allocation is removed as conditions clear.

    Mode transitions

    What is always allowed, what requires a gate

    Economy and Hills/Terrain can be selected freely in any operating state except Turtle (State 5) and refuel recovery (State 6), where mode changes are deferred until Turtle exits. Sport requires ≥20% displayed SoC, valid BMS evidence, available discharge power, and no active fault — and can be refused if any of these is absent.

    Always permitted

    • Economy → Hills/Terrain
    • Sport → Economy
    • Sport → Hills/Terrain
    • Hills/Terrain → Economy

    Requires gate check

    • Economy → Sport: ≥20% SoC + BMS capability
    • Hills/Terrain → Sport: ≥20% SoC + BMS capability
    • Any mode change in State 5/6: deferred until exit

    All transitions are bumpless. The active wheel torque is preserved through the transition and only the generator calibration and filter constants change. No torque step is produced by a mode change.

    Design brief

    What the pack has to do — and what it does not

    The battery pack in a series REEV has a fundamentally different duty profile from the pack in a pure BEV. A BEV pack must store every kilowatt-hour the vehicle will ever use on a given trip. The REEV pack needs to do three things well: carry the daily city cycle on electricity alone, absorb and deliver transient power that the generator cannot ramp to fast enough, and maintain a protected Turtle reserve for emergency mobility. Highway endurance is the generator's responsibility.

    This narrower brief allows a pack roughly half the size of the XEV 9S's 79 kWh unit — but it does not mean a simpler pack. REEV duty is actually harder on cells than BEV steady-discharge duty: the generator charges the pack at up to 105 kW while the traction motor simultaneously draws from it. Charge and discharge can overlap within a single second. The chosen cell chemistry must handle this combined duty at Indian ambient temperatures across the vehicle's lifetime without degrading the Turtle reserve — the one energy window that must never shrink below its design target.

    Target usable capacities: 40 kWh RWD · 50 kWh AWD. Protected Turtle reserve: 3.0–3.2 kWh RWD (≈ 7.5–8% displayed), 3.5–4.0 kWh AWD (≈ 7–8% displayed). These are architecture targets derived from the road-load model; final values require a validated cell selection and a full BMS calibration programme.

    The hard requirement

    C-rate — why cell selection is not trivial

    The pack size is dictated by the vehicle mission, but the power requirement is dictated by the motor calibration. The traction inverter must be able to draw full calibrated output from whichever pack is fitted, and that ratio — peak power divided by usable energy — is demanding precisely because the packs are small.

    RWD · 40 kWh pack

    ≈ 4.67C
    187 kW DC ÷ 40 kWh

    Full 170 kW motor output at 92% drivetrain efficiency plus 2 kW auxiliaries = 187 kW battery DC demand. Divided by 40 kWh usable: 4.67C peak. A comparable energy-optimised Blade 1.0 LFP cell built for the 79 kWh BEV duty runs at around 2.37C for the same motor.

    AWD · 50 kWh pack

    ≈ 5.26C
    263 kW DC ÷ 50 kWh

    170 kW rear + 70 kW front = 240 kW combined at 92% efficiency plus auxiliaries = 263 kW DC demand. Divided by 50 kWh: 5.26C peak. Pack sizes below 48 kWh were evaluated and set aside — at 44 kWh the AWD pack reaches 5.98C, at 40 kWh it reaches 6.58C. Those figures are in the borderline zone for LFP chemistry under Indian summer temperatures, where sustained 40°C+ ambient and heavy air-conditioning combine with repeated highway pulls. The front EDU adds abrupt burst demands on top of sustained rear-drive load; the pack must absorb both simultaneously, not alternately.

    Both packs demand validated high-power cells. The 79 kWh INGLO Blade 1.0 cell, which runs at a comfortable 2.4C peak for its own vehicle, cannot be used at 40 kWh — the C-rate roughly doubles and the cell was never characterised for it. This is the core reason cell family selection is a non-trivial engineering decision for this concept.

    Why these specific pack sizes — the C-rate alignment argument

    The peak power demands are set by the motor calibration: 187 kW RWD, 263 kW AWD. The ratio between them is fixed at 1.406. For equal C-rates, the AWD pack must always be 1.406× larger than the RWD pack — and that ratio points directly at the 40/50 kWh pair.

    At 40 kWh RWD and 50 kWh AWD, both packs land at 4.67C and 5.26C respectively — close enough that a single Short Blade 2.0 high-power cell family can serve both variants with different Ah ratings in the same prismatic format. This is standard BYD practice across PHEV/REEV pack families. The 40/50 kWh split is therefore not an arbitrary round number: it is the smallest pair of round kWh values at which both variants stay within 5.3C and the C-rate asymmetry between the two is minimal (0.59C delta).

    The 400V constraint reinforces this. Both packs use a 108-series cell string at 3.8 V nominal, giving a ~410 V nominal bus. Changing pack energy without changing series count means changing cell Ah — which changes the current at peak power (187 kW ÷ 410 V = ~456 A RWD; 263 kW ÷ 410 V = ~641 A AWD). Going to a 2-parallel string to reduce C-rate on AWD doubles the pack to ~71 kWh and adds approximately 150 kg — clearly counterproductive. There is no route to a lower AWD C-rate inside the 400V, 1p architecture without accepting a much larger pack.

    The certified MIDC range — ~340 km RWD and ~408 km AWD — is a consequence of these electrically-correct pack sizes, not a design input. The AWD's range headroom is not over-engineering: it is the range that results from giving the 263 kW drive system a healthy 5.26C operating margin.

    Cell family rationale

    Why Short Blade 2.0 — and why the chemistry is deliberately left open

    BYD officially announced Blade 2.0 and its flash-charging performance in March 2026. Public secondary analysis describes the Short Blade 2.0 as capable of very high charge/discharge rates, improved low-temperature behaviour, lower resistance, and improved thermal integration. Some launch reports describe an LMFP cathode at approximately 3.8 V nominal. However, early production regulatory and technical analysis still describes LFP. BYD's public launch material does not provide the complete cathode, cell-voltage, Ah, resistance, or power map needed for design work.

    This specification therefore says "Short Blade 2.0 high-power phosphate cell" — not confirmed LMFP. LMFP, 3.8 V nominal, 8–10C charging, 16C pulse discharge, 190–210 Wh/kg, and 4,000-cycle figures remain supplier-confirmation items, not design constants. The architecture is evaluated for both the LFP case and the conditional LMFP case below, because the voltage difference between them changes the series cell count needed to stay within the INGLO 400–465 V window.

    Why the power requirement drives cell family, not energy density

    The main benefit of Short Blade 2.0 is power capability — lower internal resistance, higher sustained C-rate, and better pulse repetition — not a promise of dramatically more range from the same 40/50 kWh. The Blade 1.0 is an excellent cell for the 79 kWh BEV duty it was designed for. It is not designed for 4.67C peak discharge from a 40 kWh pack. The Short Blade 2.0 platform's PHEV-class variant is built for exactly the shallow-cycle, high-pulse-power, repeated-generator-charge duty this application demands.

    Supplier and ecosystem continuity

    Public analysis identifies BYD/FinDreams Blade LFP cells in the production INGLO 59/79 kWh packs. The Short Blade 2.0 is the next generation from the same family. Choosing this lineage is more grounded than specifying an unrelated cell, pack geometry, and service ecosystem. It also means the same tooling partner, similar module integration philosophy, and a supplier relationship Mahindra already has or can extend — relevant for an INGLO-derivative programme.

    Partial-SoC cycling and Turtle reserve integrity

    The REEV pack spends most of its working life between 15% and 80% SoC — shallow cycling, not full-depth. BEV packs are stress-tested on full-depth cycles; REEV packs age by a different mechanism. The Turtle reserve floor (3.0–4.0 kWh) must remain available at end of warranty. Both LFP and LMFP phosphate variants retain capacity well under hot partial-SoC cycling. NMC variants do not, at Indian ambient conditions.

    Thermal stability at Indian ambient

    Peak summer ambient in the Indo-Gangetic Plain exceeds 45°C. A parked vehicle in direct sun can see pack temperatures above 50°C before driving begins. Phosphate olivine crystal structure is stable to above 270°C thermal runaway onset — this is independent of whether the cathode is LFP or LMFP. The pack can sit charged in a hot Indian parking lot without accelerated calendar degradation. This is a structural advantage over both NMC variants and removes a cooling-system constraint those chemistries impose.

    Voltage architecture

    Two cell-count cases — both stay within the INGLO 400–465 V window

Moving to a 1,000 V bus would require a new motor/inverter, OBC, DC/DC, compressor, contactors, insulation system, charging inlet, and either a newly wound high-voltage generator or a ~105 kW boost converter — incompatible with maximum INGLO reuse. The voltage architecture is therefore fixed; what changes between the two chemistry cases is the series cell count needed to land in the same window.

Exact series count, cell Ah, maximum charge voltage, module/CTP geometry, and BMS topology stay open until a production Short Blade 2.0 datasheet is nominated.

    Thermal management

    What phosphate chemistry buys — and what still requires engineering

    Both LFP and LMFP share the olivine crystal structure that gives phosphate cells their thermal runaway threshold above 270°C. This is independent of which cathode variant Short Blade 2.0 production confirms. The implication for system design is the same in either case: the worst-case thermal runaway propagation risk is structurally lower than any NMC pack, and the cell does not require preconditioning before charging in Indian summer conditions.

    • Cooling strategy: bottom-plate liquid cooling carried over from the INGLO platform. The flat Blade 2.0 geometry suits direct bottom-plate contact with minimal thermal interface resistance, consistent with BYD's cell-to-pack integration approach.
    • Combined charge/discharge heat load: the REEV duty cycle adds a sustained heat source the XEV 9S never sees — generator charging the pack at 30–105 kW while traction simultaneously draws from it. The cooling system must be sized for this combined duty, not just for peak discharge. Generator peak heat rejection at 105 kW output is approximately 11–13 kW (at 89.7% conversion efficiency), routed through a separate heat exchanger on the same chiller circuit.
    • Park thermal soak: phosphate stability means preconditioning is advisory rather than mandatory for a hot-soak start. The BMS may briefly reduce peak charge acceptance after prolonged hot parking, but the Turtle reserve and normal discharge capability are unaffected.
    • Cold start: both LFP and LMFP show reduced charge acceptance below 5°C. Generator recovery states (S6) already limit charging to 30–40 kW; the BMS charge-acceptance authority clips this further if cell temperature requires it.
    • Full pulse power ceiling: the design targets full pulse power below approximately 45°C, with progressive derating beginning around 45–50°C. 55–60°C is not a normal operating target; supplier thermal maps are authoritative.

    Pack thermal architecture, fluid flow rates, chiller sizing, and the generator-to-pack heat load integration are detailed in the Engineering Record alongside other open programme items.

    What this pack does not solve

    Open items and honest limits

    The cell selection and pack architecture above are directionally correct but not closed. The following remain open before any hardware could be committed:

    • Final cell selection: supplier engagement, datasheet validation, and sample testing against the REEV charge/discharge duty profile at 40–45°C ambient.
    • Series/parallel topology: exact cell count, blade length, module form factor, and BMS addressability — all require a CAD fit-check against the freed INGLO floor section.
    • BMS calibration: the Turtle reserve floor values (3.0–4.0 kWh) are derived from the road-load model; they require physical validation against the weakest-cell model and end-of-life capacity retention at operating temperature.
    • Cycle life confirmation: the 2,500-cycle partial-depth estimate is derived from LMFP published data. Warranty period, odometer target, and fleet-use patterns determine whether additional parallel strings (to reduce per-cell DoD) are needed.
    • Regulatory compliance: AIS 038 Rev 2, AIS 156 (battery safety), and homologation against the applicable Indian BMS tamper-protection and isolation requirements.

    Architecture

    Series topology — engine never drives the wheels

    The combustion engine has no mechanical path to either axle. Wheel torque is always electric, supplied by the PMSM traction motor through a fixed-ratio reduction gear. The engine drives only the generator, which rectifies AC to HV DC and feeds the shared battery bus. This separation is the design's central property: the engine can be calibrated as a fixed-band electrical source rather than a variable-speed traction actuator, and the driver never feels engine load changes as torque steps.

    The engine-generator unit is crankshaft-coupled to a permanent-magnet synchronous generator (PMSG). The PMSG output is rectified to the 400–465 V INGLO-class HV DC bus, the same bus that the battery, traction inverter, onboard charger, and DC/DC auxiliary converter all share. There is no mechanical clutch, no generator-only propulsion mode, and no second energy source that can bypass the traction battery. Generator power that exceeds immediate demand charges the battery; battery power that exceeds available generator supply fills the traction gap.

    Power reference planes: The six generator bands state usable HV DC-bus output after generator and rectifier losses, but before traction-inverter, motor, and reduction-gear losses. Crankshaft power is approximately 11.5% higher at the provisional conversion efficiency. Do not mix these planes in requirements, telemetry, or CAN messages.

    Setup diagram

    Power flow from fuel tank to wheels

    The HV DC bus is the single shared link. All sources and loads connect to it; no source connects directly to any other source. The generator, battery, and onboard charger can each charge the bus; the traction inverter, DC/DC auxiliary converter, and V2L outlet each draw from it. The onboard charger is bidirectional: the same physical charge port accepts inbound AC in one session and delivers 230 V AC export in another, with no second port or dedicated export inverter required.

    Engine

    1.5 litre mStallion TGDi — generator-derivative calibration

    The base engine is the 1.5 litre mStallion TGDi petrol unit, rated at approximately 163 hp / 121.5 kW peak and 280 Nm in its production traction form. The generator-derivative concept adapts this engine for a fixed-band electrical role: no mechanical wheel connection, no transient traction demand, and no gear-dependent speed/load sweep. Instead, every released operating point is pre-validated for RPM, BMEP, boost, injection, spark, cooled-EGR rate, valve timing, lambda, catalyst state, and transition dwell at each of the six output bands.

    The intended derivative uses an expansion-biased Miller/Atkinson-like VVT strategy, cooled EGR, direct injection, and a fixed-ratio direct generator coupling. It does not assume an active pre-chamber, spark-assisted compression ignition, or lambda-2 operation across the full map. Modest lean operation is applied only in the lower bands where combustion stability and emissions compliance permit it. The revised provisional BTE figures in the combustion table are informed by the Jaecoo 44.5% dual-duty benchmark and the structural generator-only calibration advantage.

    The 42-litre petrol tank is shared between RWD and AWD. Both variants use the same engine, generator, after-treatment, control strategy, and service parts wherever packaging permits.

    Long-term derivative direction. The baseline uses the production 1.5 litre mStallion TGDi — four cylinders of ~375 cc each. Mahindra also produces the 2.0 litre mStallion TGDi — four cylinders of ~500 cc each — in the current petrol ICE powertrain. The proposed derivative takes those larger 500 cc cylinder specifications and builds a purpose-specific three-cylinder 1.5 litre unit: three cylinders of ~500 cc each, the same total displacement but a fundamentally different combustion geometry. A 500 cc cylinder has a lower surface-to-volume ratio than a 375 cc cylinder, which means less heat rejected to coolant per cycle and a direct path to higher BTE — without changing displacement. Combined with a dedicated high-expansion-ratio Miller cycle, cooled EGR, and split injection with spray-guided stratification — an early intake-stroke injection laying a homogeneous base charge, followed by a late compression injection creating a locally rich ignitable zone near the plug for stable lean combustion at the 30–55 kW bands, with a single homogeneous early injection at the upper lambda-1 bands — this derivative would push BTE materially beyond the revised figures in the table above, particularly at the lower lean-capable bands. Nissan's e-POWER programme is the production proof of the same principle: the original Note e-POWER uses a 1.2 litre three-cylinder generator engine; the Qashqai and X-Trail e-POWER use a 1.5 litre three-cylinder VC-Turbo, for which Nissan claims approximately 50% thermal efficiency in generator-only duty — a figure enabled by variable compression ratio technology unique to that engine. The relevant takeaway is not the 50% headline but the confirmation that a purpose-built three-cylinder generator engine at fixed operating points outperforms a general-duty engine of the same displacement on BTE. This derivative reaches for the same efficiency region through larger per-cylinder geometry, a dedicated Miller cycle, and split injection with spray-guided stratification rather than variable compression ratio — staying within existing combustion hardware categories. It requires a purpose-built engine programme and is outside the current concept scope, recorded here as a documented second-generation opportunity.

    Band selection logic

    Why fixed bands rather than continuous throttle

    A conventional wheel-driven petrol engine must cover launch, shifting, rapid pedal demand, gear-dependent speed/load points, and transient boost response — all in real time. The series architecture removes every one of those constraints. The engine can pre-validate its operating points and dwell at each one until a condition change is confirmed. This gives precise control over RPM, combustion quality, catalyst temperature, NOx storage state, and ramp rate — the variables that determine emissions, noise, and efficiency — at every moment the generator runs.

    The controller selects the smallest band whose steady HV DC output covers sustained traction demand, auxiliary load, and the battery recovery target for the active mode. The upshift dwell prevents hunting on a rising load; the downshift dwell (measured from when demand fell, not from the last band change) prevents premature downshift at the crest of a climb. During braking, the mechanical engine band is held for the 12-second dwell for driveability. The rectifier stays active — the 400V bus is sized for 270 kW (AWD peak traction), and the maximum combined generator and regen charge is 143 kW, 53% of that capacity. Both sources charge the battery simultaneously throughout every brake event. Engine mechanical behaviour and rectifier electrical output are deliberately decoupled in the control architecture, but on this vehicle the rectifier never needs to unload.

    How to read the bands: generator output is one source on a shared bus. Road load, battery capability, inverter and motor limits, and the active operating state together determine what the vehicle can do at any moment. The bands describe generator contribution; performance comes from the system.

    Regenerative braking

    Braking returns energy — but the bus sets the ceiling

    Every braking event that does not need maximum hydraulic friction feeds kinetic energy back into the HV DC bus as electrical charge. The traction motor runs as a generator during deceleration. The regen ceiling is 30 kW RWD and 40 kW AWD: above that, the mechanical hydraulic friction brakes carry the remainder.

    The controlling constraint is live BMS charge approval. Both variants share the 400V bus architecture sized for 270 kW — the AWD peak traction demand of ~263 kW DC sets the bus current rating (~675 A), and BMS charge acceptance is bounded by the same architecture. The absolute worst case this vehicle can deliver to the battery is 105 kW generator + 40 kW regen − 2 kW aux = 143 kW combined, which is 53% of the 270 kW bus capacity. The rectifier unload condition — combined generator charge plus regen exceeding BMS charge acceptance — is physically unreachable on this vehicle under any operating combination. The interlock logic remains in the control law for correctness, but it does not fire in practice.

    Rule: The generator holds its mechanical band for 12 s after braking begins for driveability — the engine sound does not change abruptly on a light brake press. The rectifier stays active: the 270 kW bus architecture means no combination of generator band and regeneration on this vehicle approaches the charge acceptance limit. Both sources charge the battery simultaneously throughout every brake event.

    Band held during descent

    In Hills/Terrain mode on a gradient above 3% with active braking, the generator pauses rather than holds its band. On the descent the battery absorbs regen at maximum approval; once braking ends and the road climbs again, the generator resumes. On a sustained descent lasting longer than the downshift dwell, the generator steps down through bands rather than holding — the dwell timer counts from when demand fell, not from when the gradient eased.

    The 70% round-trip capture efficiency is the planning figure used consistently across the running-costs model and the simulator. What that figure would take to certify is covered in the Engineering Record.

    Auxiliary load priority

    What gets protected — and what gets trimmed — when energy is scarce

    All auxiliaries — HVAC, lighting, audio, power outlets, cooling pumps, steering, brakes — draw from the shared HV DC bus via the DC/DC auxiliary converter. There is no second source to switch to; the bus is the only supply. Priority is applied by reducing or shedding loads in a fixed order as operating states advance toward Turtle, not by switching sources.

    A0 — always on, never shed
    BMS, VCU, contactor logic, pre-charge, isolation monitoring, crash detection, 12 V vital bus. Losing any of these ends propulsion safely. They stay on from first HV closure to final safe-park discharge, including all recovery states.
    A1 — safety and legal minimum — never shed for energy
    ABS/ESC/EBD, EPAS, brake booster pump, forward lighting, hazard flashers, rearward lighting, horn, emergency 4G/V2X, seat-belt pretensioner supply. These can only be lost to an active fault. Energy shortage alone never sheds them.
    A2 — thermal management — reduced but not zero
    Battery thermal management, motor cooling, generator cooling, and after-treatment keep-warm. Cooling flow rates may reduce in State 4 to cut parasitic draw, but they can never reach zero while a component is within its operating temperature window. A thermal alert overrides any shedding decision.
    A3 — cabin comfort — trimmed only in State 5 (Turtle)
    HVAC compressor demand is trimmed only in State 5. The trim is a kW cap, not a full shutdown. Temperature and recirculation targets may not be reached, but the fan continues. In State 5 only keep-alive ventilation runs unless outside temperature is hazardous. Restored immediately when fuel is detected and generator recovery begins (State 6).
    A4 — convenience — shed only in State 5 (Turtle)
    Seat heaters, steering-wheel heater, rear heated screen, power outlets, ambient lighting, audio amplifier standby. These shed only on entry to State 5. Their combined draw is 1.5–2.5 kW and recovering it is the lowest-cost restoration action available. Restored immediately when fuel is detected and generator recovery begins (State 6).
    A5 — battery recovery surplus — can go to zero
    The kW surplus allocated to charging the battery above the traction-and-auxiliaries floor. This is always the last to be served and can reach zero in State 4 if generator power is exactly consumed by A0–A4 and traction. The battery does not discharge for traction in State 4, but it also may not receive charge if no generator surplus remains after higher-priority loads.

    No physical switch between auxiliary sources exists. The HV DC bus and its DC/DC converter are the uninterrupted supply for every priority level. The battery and generator share the bus; neither is a dedicated auxiliary source. This means a generator that supplies 30 kW in State 3 is simultaneously providing traction, cooling, HVAC, and surplus charge, all from the same bus.

    Vehicle Control Unit

    The decision layer — LV network, no direct HV connection

    The VCU is the sole arbitrator of powertrain state. It lives on the low-voltage CAN network and has no direct electrical connection to the HV bus. Every energy flow on the bus is a consequence of a VCU command issued to a bus participant — the bus itself carries out no arbitration logic. This separation is architectural: the VCU can be isolated, reset, or replaced without touching any HV circuit.

    The VCU runs the state machine described in the How it thinks tab — States 1 through 6 — and translates each state into commands to four bus participants. Nothing on the HV bus acts on driver input directly; all driver requests pass through the VCU first.

    Safety hardware on the bus

    • Main contactors (positive and negative): isolate battery from bus; opened on any HV fault, crash signal, or HVIL break.
    • Pre-charge contactor + resistor: limits inrush into inverter and OBC capacitors at bus energisation; main positive contactor closes only after pre-charge completes.
    • HVIL loop: continuous circuit through every HV connector; any connector break opens the main contactors immediately.
    • Pack fuse (pyrotechnic or blade): upstream of the positive contactor; one-shot protection against bus short circuit.
    • Generator rectifier contactor: separates rectifier from bus; opened during DC fast charge sessions, closed whenever the generator is running.
    • Isolation monitoring: continuous DC isolation resistance measurement between the HV bus and chassis; faults are published to BMS and powertrain controller immediately.
    Generator inhibited during DC fast charge: Two active sources on the same bus require arbitration that is only well-defined with the generator off. When a DC fast charge session is active, the rectifier contactor is opened and the generator is inhibited. This is not a limitation — combined charge to battery never exceeds 143 kW (gen + regen), which is 53% of the 270 kW bus ceiling. The bus ceiling exists for traction, not charging.

    AC charging and DC charging

    Standard INGLO charging — no additional hardware

    The same bidirectional OBC that handles V2L export handles inbound AC charging. The onboard charger connects the AC supply to the HV DC bus; the battery, generator, traction inverter, and auxiliary converter share that bus. AC charging rate is governed by the OBC's rated power and live BMS charge approval. The INGLO platform supports up to 11 kW AC (three-phase) on the XEV 9e variant; the REEV concept targets a compatible rate.

    Public DC fast charging bypasses the OBC entirely: the DC charger connects directly to the HV DC bus via the CCS2/Bharat DC-001 port. The battery's charge approval is the only active gate. The generator is inhibited during DC charging for HV safety — two active sources on the same bus require arbitration that is only well-defined with the generator off.

    The 400–465 V bus architecture is sized for 270 kW, set by AWD peak traction demand (~263 kW DC). That internal bus capacity is not the external charging ceiling. The CCS2 connector is rated to ~500 A; at 400 V nominal that limits DC fast charge to approximately 200 kW, rising to ~232 kW at peak pack voltage (~465 V). This concept targets a 150–180 kW practical DC fast charge rate — achievable within the connector and bus limits, compatible with current Indian public charging infrastructure, and well within the 500 A ceiling. The upper design target is 240 kW at peak pack voltage (~465 V) with a compatible charger; this approaches ~516 A and requires charger-side current capability confirmation, but the bus architecture supports it and the Short Blade 2.0 high-power cell is rated for sustained high charge rates. The 270 kW internal bus headroom exists because peak traction demands it, not because external charging reaches it.

    Grid-to-vehicle and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) participation, smart-tariff scheduling, and ISO 15118 Plug & Charge are not addressed in this concept. The bidirectional OBC is a necessary but not sufficient hardware precondition for V2G. The control, communication, utility interface, and regulatory requirements for true grid participation are outside scope.

    V2L architecture

    One port, one bidirectional OBC, two operating modes

    The INGLO platform already carries a bidirectional onboard charger (OBC), demonstrated on the XEV 9e. The same hardware that accepts inbound AC for grid charging reverses current direction at session start to export 230 V AC. There is no second port, no dedicated export inverter, and no additional HV hardware. The charge port, cable, and six mandatory interlocks are the same for inbound charging and outbound export — only the current direction and the session floor condition differ.

    The HV DC bus is the common point. All power flow — generator output, battery discharge, traction demand, regeneration, and V2L export — connects to the same bus. During a V2L session, the export load is treated as a critical auxiliary: it has first claim on surplus generator power in Emergency Rescue mode, and on battery discharge power in Home Battery Backup mode. The traction inverter is locked out in Park; the bus powers the OBC for export and the DC/DC for vehicle auxiliaries.

    Home Battery Backup — how it works

    Bounded UPS function for a household

    When the vehicle is parked with a healthy SoC and a power cut occurs, the owner connects the vehicle to a standard household plug via the export cable. The OBC reverses current direction. Export begins immediately. No generator starts, no exhaust, no noise — the vehicle is a silent battery pack. The household draws from the traction battery through the HV DC bus and DC/DC via the OBC.

    The session ends automatically when the battery reaches 15% displayed SoC — the Economy generator-start floor. Below that, the generator would normally start, but the home backup mode prohibits it unconditionally. The owner does not need to monitor or disconnect; the vehicle stops export cleanly, returns to standby, and the generator never starts. This makes unattended overnight operation safe.

    Sizing

    • RWD 40 kWh usable, from 100% to 15% SoC: approximately 34 kWh available for export before losses.
    • At 3.3 kW maximum export: approximately 10 hours of supply from full charge.
    • At a typical Indian household load of 1.0–1.5 kW (ceiling fans, refrigerator, LED lighting, WiFi router): approximately 17–25 hours from full charge.
    • AWD 50 kWh usable: approximately 42 kWh available, extending both figures proportionally.

    These are planning estimates at nominal ambient temperature, healthy pack, and stable load. BMS charge/discharge efficiency, conversion losses in the OBC, idle auxiliary draw (BMS, cooling pumps, communication), and degraded capacity in an aged pack will reduce usable export. A vehicle at 80% of nominal capacity delivers approximately 27 kWh RWD to the household before the floor, not 34 kWh.

    Emergency Vehicle Rescue — how it works

    Restoring a depleted EV from another vehicle's generator

    A donor REEV with sufficient SoC and validated fuel connects to the depleted recipient EV's AC charge port via the export cable. The export load is treated as a critical auxiliary. As the donor's battery draws down through the export session, the generator starts at the active mode boundary to sustain export power from fuel rather than from donor battery energy. The generator's output minus auxiliaries is surplus that both charges the donor battery and supplies the export.

    A 3.3 kW supply at the recipient's port, sustained for 10 minutes, delivers approximately 0.55 kWh before losses. At 20 minutes, approximately 1.1 kWh. Recovery ends when the recipient's battery has enough pulse power to crank its own generator — it does not need to reach a displayed percentage, only enough kWh for a controlled generator start sequence.

    Rule: The donor vehicle must be in open air with adequate ventilation. The driver must acknowledge the exhaust warning before the session begins. The session may not be started in an enclosed space. Departure is confirmed only after safe disconnection, Park, READY, brake-applied, no-connector interlock checks, and fuel-validated generator-start capability.

    Six mandatory interlocks

    Same six gates for both modes — only the floor condition differs

    Every V2L session — whether Home Battery Backup or Emergency Rescue — must pass the same six conditions before export begins. These are not soft warnings; any one of them failing prevents the session from starting and terminates an active session immediately.

    1. Gear selector in Park — propulsion locked out; the traction inverter cannot engage while export is active.
    2. Vehicle READY state confirmed — HV bus live, contactors closed, BMS publishing valid limits.
    3. Charge connector seated and pilot signal validated — the cable and socket are confirmed before HV appears at the port.
    4. No active isolation fault, contactor fault, pre-charge fault, or pack thermal event — the battery must be electrically healthy; a safety fault prohibits any bidirectional HV flow.
    5. SoC above the applicable floor — Home Backup: above 15%; Emergency Rescue: above the dynamic Turtle boundary. Checks live during the session; export terminates when the floor is crossed.
    6. Exhaust acknowledgement (Emergency Rescue only) — driver must confirm open-air placement before the generator starts. The Home Backup session omits this interlock because the generator is unconditionally inhibited.

    What it is

    REEV is not a new idea. What this concept brings together is.

    Nissan put fixed-point generator operation and always-electric drive into volume production in 2016 with the Note e-Power — validating the engine-as-powerplant principle, though the e-Power carries only a small buffer battery and is never grid-charged. Li Auto, Leapmotor, and Aito extended that proof into the plug-in REEV at scale in China, adding a large usable battery and grid charging. The architecture itself — engine drives only a generator, wheels are always electric — is established and validated. The question this concept pursues is different: what does a REEV look like when every subsystem is designed to work together rather than assembled from available parts?

    The answer is a specific combination that does not exist in a single production vehicle today. A right-sized 40/50 kWh phosphate pack — large enough for meaningful daily EV range and high-power C-rates, small enough to keep cost and mass in check. A generator sized at 105 kW, genuinely capable of sustained highway support rather than a token emergency unit. Six pre-validated fixed operating bands with a smallest-sufficient selection law, calibrated for efficiency and NVH rather than chasing the accelerator. A control law where mode logic, generator hysteresis, battery protection, auxiliary shedding, regen acceptance, and low-energy recovery are all treated as one interacting system — not bolted together from independent subsystems. And all of it on an existing production platform — Mahindra's INGLO skateboard — without a clean-sheet vehicle or an 800 V architecture.

    The core product is neither an ICE SUV carrying an assist battery nor a BEV carrying a token emergency engine. It is an EV-first, plug-in, always-electric-drive SUV. Every kilometre is driven electrically. The engine never touches the axles.

    When a BEV is the better fit

    A buyer with reliable home and destination charging and a predictable daily range is better served by a pure BEV: simpler, fewer failure modes, and normally cheaper to run. The target here is the large middle market that wants mostly grid-electric daily travel and electric driving character while keeping the option to refuel quickly on a long or unplanned trip. Those two markets are different and both are real.

    Where it sits

    The category is already proven by others

    Li Auto, Leapmotor, Aito and others have validated the global EREV category: substantial plug-in batteries, electric-only wheel propulsion, engine-generator range extension and strong performance are credible production directions today. This concept builds on that validation. The question it pursues is whether a Mahindra-sized platform, India-first cost assumptions, and a specific generator and control law can produce a compelling EREV at the price and duty cycle that matters for the Indian market.

    Why the factory cost gap is narrow: The REEV sits on the INGLO BEV skateboard — no gearbox. The Tomahawk sits on the Hector monocoque with a DHT in place of a conventional AT. Three items cancel nearly perfectly: the gearbox deletion (saves ₹0.8–1.5 lakh), replacing the DHT's generation function with a standalone PMSG generator (saves ₹0.5–0.8 lakh), offset by a separate PMSM EDU vs the DHT's integrated traction motor (costs ₹0.3–0.6 lakh more) and the 20 kWh additional battery at scale LFP pricing (costs ₹1.0–1.4 lakh more).     How the control law behaves during that test

    The 15 percent Economy and 20 percent Sport and Hills values are generator-management anchors, not EV discharge limits. With generator power unavailable, normal EV operation continues through the 10 percent intervention region, battery Turtle begins at its dynamic energy target of provisionally 7 to 8 percent, and the vehicle continues to displayed 0 percent for as long as it can safely follow the prescribed cycle.

    The protected physical 3 percent remains excluded from measured and advertised range. Turtle power must stay sufficient to follow the cycle, because reclassifying energy between normal operation and Turtle does not change total range. Only an inability to follow the cycle before displayed 0 percent would lose usable energy and threaten the 270 km result.

      Group A

      Assumptions and outstanding work

      These are the claims that require physical test, supplier data or regulatory agreement to close. Each entry states what would establish it.

      Pack and cell work outstanding

      Cell chemistry, series/parallel topology, voltage and current maps, pulse durability, balancing, contactor control, and pack thermal models remain supplier, HIL and physical-test work. The 40 kWh and 50 kWh usable figures are architecture targets, not validated cell counts. Establishing them requires a supplier cell selection, a pack design, and a full validation programme against the road-load demand profile.

      Cell nomination boundary: BYD's public launch material for Blade 2.0 (March 2026) is sufficient to identify this as the preferred cell family for investigation. It is not sufficient to release voltage, current, cooling, charging, or warranty calibrations. The final cell selection requires a nominated production Short Blade 2.0 datasheet, a complete pack power map over SoC, temperature, ageing, imbalance, pulse length, repetition, cooling state, and voltage sag — and a Mahindra supplier nomination.

      Pack thermal architecture: fluid flow rates, chiller sizing, and the generator-to-pack heat load integration are open engineering items. The battery tab describes design direction and constraints; a completed thermal analysis requires physical testing.

      Engine and generator figures

      BTE, BSFC and BMEP values in the generator calibration table are provisional power-level abstractions derived from comparable published engine families. They are not measured mStallion TGDi figures. Establishing the six operating bands requires engine-generator dyno maps for the specific coupled unit.

      Road-load model assumed constants

      CdA of 0.89 m² is an XUV700-class estimate; Crr of 0.0095 is a standard passenger tyre assumption on dry asphalt; kerb masses of 2,300 kg RWD and 2,500 kg AWD are grounded nominal targets. All three require coast-down testing on a production-representative vehicle to confirm.

      AWD allocation

      Front motor sizing and the AWD torque split are presented as architecture illustrations, not as terrain-control software. Establishing them requires a vehicle dynamics programme and a validated AWD control strategy.

      Regeneration and certified braking

      The 70% round-trip regen recovery assumption is a planning figure. Certified brake-by-wire blending requires hardware design, ABS and ESC integration, and regulatory homologation.

      V2L export

      Two V2L operating modes are specified: home battery backup (battery-only discharge to the 15% SoC floor, generator never starts, unattended operation permitted) and emergency vehicle rescue (generator engages at the donor mode boundary). Both share the same bidirectional OBC and charge port. Neither mode is modelled in the simulation. Power output, efficiency, thermal limits, OBC reversal latency, and grid-return certification are entirely unmodelled.

      Competitive position

      The positioning section identifies Li Auto, Leapmotor and Aito as reference vehicles. Direct superiority over any of them would require like-for-like tested comparisons of mass, cost, range, charging, NVH, emissions, reliability, controls and warranty. None of those tests have been run.

      ARAI procedure and certified range

      The ~340 km RWD and ~408 km AWD MIDC estimates are derived from the road-load model with the generator disabled, calibrated against the XEV 9S 79 kWh MIDC claim of 679 km. Real-world range is estimated at 60–75% of MIDC, consistent with XEV 9S observed results (410–510 km). The applicable OVC-HEV procedure, test family and variant selection, break-off criterion and declared value all require ARAI and regulatory agreement. Actual test outcomes remain open.

      HV bus detailed design — four open items

      The bus architecture is specified at the topology, rating, source arbitration, load priority, and safety-hardware level. Four items require a physical package and supplier data before they can be closed.

      • Contactor control sequencing: pre-charge timing, interlock handshake with the READY signal, and contactor state machine under partial-fault conditions are BMS and VCU firmware work. The sequence must be validated against the specific contactor hardware and the pack's inrush characteristic.
      • Bus capacitor sizing: the inverter and OBC input-stage capacitance determine the pre-charge resistor value, the pre-charge time budget, and the bus voltage ripple under pulsed traction load. These depend on the inverter and OBC supplier's input stage design — unresolved until hardware is nominated.
      • HV cable gauge and routing: cross-sectional area, insulation class, shielding, and connector specification depend on the physical package — distances from battery to inverter, from rectifier to bus, and from CCS2 inlet to bus. Not modelled in this concept phase.
      • Generator rectifier topology: a passive diode bridge and an active synchronous rectifier both work architecturally. The active rectifier recovers a few percent of rectifier loss (diode forward drop vs active switching loss) and enables four-quadrant operation, but adds control complexity and cost. The choice affects bus-side efficiency and the contactor sequencing above. Unresolved — both are compatible with this concept's control law.

      Group B

      Reversals — where the design was wrong and changed

      These entries record positions the concept held, the evidence that broke them, and what replaced them. They cannot be produced without a real argument having happened.

      Turtle mode gate: 10% hard lockout → 8% recoverable state

      Original position: Turtle was a hard lockout at 10% displayed. Once crossed, no recovery was possible until external energy was supplied.

      What broke it: A hybrid owner who adds five litres roadside should be able to drive. A hard 10% lockout prevents generator-restart recovery even when the fuel condition is satisfied and the battery is electrically healthy.

      Replacement: Turtle became a recoverable state with fuel-detection generator restart. The gate moved to 8%, so the 10% region is now the early-intervention window where generator-limited operation holds battery level without locking the driver out.

      Terrain fallback: symmetric Economy demotion → asymmetric hold

      Original position: Both Sport and Terrain fell back to Economy power management at 10% displayed.

      What broke it: The Terrain fallback destroyed the exact capability the driver selected Terrain for. Terrain's band-hunting, descent regen priority and AWD preparation are its defining behaviours on a hill. Demoting it to Economy at the moment those capabilities are most needed is incoherent.

      Replacement: Sport still falls back to Economy at 10%, because Sport's value is peak acceleration which genuinely requires battery headroom. Terrain holds its band-hunting character from 20% down to 8%. The asymmetry is the conclusion, not an oversight.

      Generator downshift dwell: time-since-band-change → time-since-demand-fell

      Original position: The downshift dwell was measured from the last band change.

      What broke it: The executable model failed its own assertion. Time-since-band-change allows a downshift when demand has only just fallen and the engine is still decelerating, which produces the band hunting the dwell exists to prevent. The defect surfaced because the model checks the condition explicitly.

      Replacement: Dwell is measured from the moment demand fell below the current band threshold, not from the band change itself.

      Consumption model: empirical km/l constants → road-load derivation

      Original position: Running-cost figures used empirical km/l constants for each vehicle.

      What broke it: The constants could not be defended under questioning. They absorb all physics into a single number, so any change in mass, speed or payload is invisible, and there is no way to verify the number is right except by comparing it to another constant.

      Replacement: The published road-load model now drives all consumption figures. Combustion vehicle km/l values are anchored to their published reference condition and scaled by the physics-derived energy ratio at any other condition. Any reader can reproduce every figure from the model constants.

      Group C

      Proposals analysed and declined

      Supercapacitor plus surge battery

      Proposal: A supercapacitor and surge battery to cap the HV bus at 180 kW peak.

      Why declined: The 30% and 35% generator cutoffs already guarantee charge headroom for transients, so the proposal solved a problem the control law had already solved. It added mass and cost with no capability gain.

      Auxiliary supply switching by mode

      Proposal: Switch the auxiliary supply between battery and generator depending on drive mode.

      Why declined: On a common DC bus there is no second source to switch to. Battery and generator share the HV link; the LV DC/DC converter always draws from the same bus regardless of which source is generating. There is no architectural basis for the switching.

      Separate low-voltage starter battery

      Proposal: A dedicated 12V or 48V battery for dead-pack recovery, independent of the traction HV system.

      Why declined: Dead mass for a rare event. The protected physical 3% bootstrap reserve already provides generator-start energy after a valid refuel. A fully depleted pack that cannot support even the bootstrap reserve is a battery safety case, not an energy case, and a starter battery would not address it.

      Bonnet port for generator-only running with a failed pack

      Proposal: An under-bonnet port allowing generator-only propulsion when the traction battery has failed.

      Why declined: An EV-first vehicle requires a functioning battery for HV-bus stability, regeneration, transient supply, and safety monitoring. A bonnet port that bypasses this requirement would conceal a genuine traction-battery safety failure rather than address it. The architecture rejects battery-isolated generator propulsion outright.

      Lambda 2 dedicated lean burn across all bands

      Proposal: Full lean-burn operation at lambda 2 across the entire generator operating range for maximum thermal efficiency.

      Why declined: Lean-NOx aftertreatment at lambda 2 requires an LNT or SCR system that broke the cost argument. The replacement is a multi-mode map: lean-capable Miller operation in the 30 to 70 kW bands where combustion conditions permit it, and lambda-1 durability operation at 90 and 105 kW where NOx formation and three-way catalyst performance are the primary constraints.

      Direct DC connection between two vehicles for roadside rescue

      Proposal: A direct HV DC link between the donor vehicle's traction battery and the recipient's charge port for a faster field rescue.

      Why declined: Voltage mismatch between two independently charged packs, current-inrush risk at the moment of connection, pack fault propagation across the link, and the absence of isolation monitoring in a field scenario make this inherently unsafe. The hardware required to do it safely — matched voltage regulation, inrush limiting, mutual isolation monitoring — is not present in either vehicle and cannot be assembled reliably at a roadside. The AC-via-OBC path uses hardware that already exists, interlocks that are already validated, and the normal charge-port protection both vehicles carry as standard.

      Cabin forward shift — 80 to 150 mm

      Proposal: The generator-only engine bay is shorter than a conventional engine plus transmission layout by roughly 150–200 mm. That recovered length could be used to push the entire cabin forward — shortening the dashboard overhang and extending the wheelbase-to-body ratio — giving meaningfully more interior space in all three rows without changing the vehicle's external footprint.

      Why deferred (not declined): Moving the cabin forward requires a full floor-pan and body-in-white redesign — firewall position, A-pillar geometry, crash load paths, and pedal box all move together. This is structural work that cannot be done as a concept-phase annotation; it requires a clean-sheet BIW programme. The gain is real — this is one of the strongest packaging arguments for a purpose-built REEV versus a BEV conversion — and it is the right direction for a production programme. It was deferred, not dismissed.

      Production intent features enabled by this shift:

      • Centre console as the cabin master controller: All cabin electronics — climate zones, ambient lighting, seat functions, display modes, audio, and connectivity — controllable from a single centre console interface. The console becomes the authority; door panels become the satellite controllers for the adjacent seat only.
      • Seat comfort functions migrate to the adjacent door: Cooled seat, heated seat, and massage functions for each seat move to the door panel of that seat's nearest door. Driver controls on driver door, co-passenger on front passenger door, second-row left on second-row left door, second-row right on second-row right door. No reaching across the cabin.
      • Second-row tumble seats (both sides): On the current INGLO XEV 9S only the left outboard seat tumbles for third-row access; the right side does not. This concept targets both sides tumbling independently as a production intent feature of the purpose-built BIW — the specific structural changes required to enable right-side tumble are a BIW programme deliverable, not a concept-phase claim. A passenger boarding behind the front-right seat should not have to walk around the vehicle.
      • Second-row sliding: Second-row seats slide on a floor track to trade legroom between second and third rows — enabled by the flat floor that the skateboard architecture already provides and the forward cabin shift amplifies.
    • Boot space gain — proportional and direct: The current XEV 9S boot with all three rows up is negligible — practically unusable for a full seven-seat trip. Moving the cabin forward 80–150 mm shifts the rear bulkhead forward by the same amount, adding exactly 80–150 mm of boot depth. This is not a packaging optimisation; it is a geometric consequence of the shift. At the upper end of the range, boot capacity moves from marginal to genuinely usable for a family trip with all seven seats occupied.

      • Why this matters architecturally: A BEV conversion on the INGLO platform retains the original BIW proportions sized around the existing front-motor-plus-HVAC stack. A purpose-built REEV BIW can take the 150–200 mm recovered in the engine bay and apply it simultaneously to passenger cell depth and boot depth — both move together as the cabin shifts forward. This is the single largest interior packaging advantage a purpose-built REEV has over a BEV conversion on the same platform, and it is only realisable with a new BIW.

      Group D

      Technology watch — future improvements identified but not adopted

      Vimag VMSM — magnet-free motor as future traction motor

      What it is: Vimag Labs, a Bengaluru-based deep-tech startup, has developed the Virtual Magnet Synchronous Motor (VMSM) — a motor that operates entirely without rare-earth permanent magnets. Instead of fixed rotor magnets, the VMSM uses a rotating transformer to wirelessly transfer power to copper windings on the rotor, turning them into software-controlled electromagnets. Proprietary control algorithms running above 20 kHz adjust rotor magnetic flux in real time based on operating conditions. The motor is brushless and slip-ring-free — no mechanical wear points from excitation. Materials are copper, steel, and standard electronics; no rare-earth sourcing dependency.

      Why it matters for this concept: A PMSM is most efficient near its rated torque and speed window; efficiency drops at light load and at the extremes of the RPM range. The VMSM reports improved torque at low speeds and sustained efficiency at higher speeds — a flat-efficiency profile across the full operating envelope. The REEV traction duty cycle is more varied than a BEV: city light load, sustained highway cruise, and full-power AWD launches within the same trip. A flat-efficiency motor extracts more usable range from the same 40/50 kWh pack at every point on that curve, not just at peak load. The rare-earth independence is an additional structural advantage for an India-first vehicle — PMSM rare-earth supply is heavily concentrated and price-volatile.

      Current validated scale: Vimag's physical prototypes as of 2026 deliver 6 kW continuous and 10 kW peak at 48–58 Nm — sized for two-wheeler applications. The company is at TRL 8 for this two-wheeler scale. Pilot verification programmes with Indian two-wheeler and passenger car manufacturers, and an unnamed European firm, are underway. The company is actively engineering heavy-duty variants in the 200–600 kW range for commercial and industrial applications, but no automotive passenger-car-scale dynometer validation at the 170 kW rear or 70 kW front ratings required by this concept has been publicly confirmed.

      Careful observation: TRL 8 is for the two-wheeler application. The jump from 10 kW prototype to 170 kW automotive traction involves significant thermal, structural, and controls engineering that has not yet been publicly validated. Pilot programmes with car manufacturers are a meaningful signal, but they are not a production commitment. Adoption in a REEV production programme would require automotive-scale validation, INGLO inverter compatibility, and supplier certification — none of which are concept-phase deliverables.

      Status: Technology watch. The PMSM EDU from the INGLO platform is the correct baseline for this concept. VMSM or equivalent magnet-free motor technology is identified as a potential future substitution — one that would improve real-world EV range, reduce rare-earth supply chain exposure, and require no change to the battery, generator, or control law if the motor interface contract is preserved.
